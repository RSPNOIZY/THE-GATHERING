## CHAPTER ONE: THE BOY WHO HEARD EVERYTHING

Ottawa. Some years ago. Before the archive. Before the industry. Before the injury. Before NOIZY.

A boy who heard the world differently.

Not better. Not worse. *Differently.* Music was not entertainment to him. It was information. It was structure. It was the way some truths traveled — truths that couldn't be said in words, that needed carrier frequencies to move from one human nervous system to another.

He heard the television. He heard the radio. He heard the space between notes. He heard the emotional intention underneath the production.

He decided he would spend his life working with sound.

This is not an unusual decision for a person who loves music. What was unusual was the precision of the commitment. Not *I want to be famous.* Not *I want to make money.* Not *I want to be a rock star.*

**I want to build things with sound that do something real to people.**

He was right about this. He was right about this from the beginning. The entire story that follows is the story of that commitment being tested — by the industry, by the market, by the machines, by the body itself — and surviving every test.

## CHAPTER FOUR: THE BODY SPEAKS

C3.

For those who don't know what C3 means: the third cervical vertebra. High on the spine. The area where injury changes not just movement but the entire architecture of a person's relationship to their body.

The diving accident.

In a single moment, the physical vocabulary that Rob had used for forty years — the hands on the board, the fingers on the keys, the arm reaching for the fader — was renegotiated. The body presented new terms. The body said: *adapt or stop.*

He adapted.

GORUNFREE was not a branding exercise. It was a survival architecture. The system he built to continue working — 35% voice, 65% AI, 1-click execution, zero friction between imagination and reality — was built because the alternative was silence.

And Rob Plowman does not do silence.

The custom input system. The voice commands. The workflow redesigned from the ground up around what the body could do, not what it used to do. The discovery, somewhere in the middle of the adaptation, that the new system was not worse. That it was, in some ways, *more*. That constraint, properly worked, becomes architecture.

The injury that should have ended the story became the prototype for the platform.

Because if you can build a system that lets a man with a C3 injury run a full creative production environment using his voice and 35% human effort — then you have built something that every human being with a body, which is to say every human being, can use to close the gap between what they imagine and what they make.
