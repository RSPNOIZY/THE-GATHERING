## 1. The lie big tech tells

Big tech tells creators a simple story:

> "Innovate. Upload. Share. We'll handle the rest."

They say this is about **possibility**. What it looks like in practice is:

- **Scrape first.**
  Your work is taken, ingested, and used as training material before you even know it's happening.
- **Sort out rights later.**
  Consent, licensing, and attribution are treated as afterthoughts, trade secrets, or legal footnotes.
- **Call it "innovation."**
  The harm is outsourced to creators: lost income, lost control, lost identity.

They frame this as progress. But it's extraction.

The lie is that the system is built for creators.
The truth is that the system is built for **scale, opacity, and profit**, with creators as the fuel.

## 2. The damage extraction causes

When platforms treat creators as extractable resources, the damage is structural, not moral.

- **Decoupling** — Sounds are stripped of their origins. You might hear a beautiful recording, but never know who made it, where it was recorded, or why it exists.
- **Black-box AI** — Your creative input is treated as training fuel. Models are built on your work, and the link back to you is severed.
- **Loss of agency** — When you can't see where your work travels or how it's reused, you lose control — and over time, you lose the safety and motivation to keep creating.

This is not just a UX problem. It's a crisis of **digital memory, dignity, and rights**.

Big tech's answer is usually:

- vague policies,
- opaque compensation,
- and a system where creators are expected to "trust" without visibility.

That's the extraction model in action.

## 2a. A concrete case: ElevenLabs and the extraction pattern

This is not a theoretical critique. There is a named, documented example of exactly what NOIZY is built against.

**ElevenLabs** is widely regarded as technically excellent at creating AI voices that sound real. But in 2024, two voice actors, two authors, and a publisher filed a lawsuit against ElevenLabs that names the extraction model precisely.

The lawsuit claims ElevenLabs trained its AI on recordings from audiobook narrations by voice actors Karissa Vacker and Mark Boyett — without their permission or compensation — to create the platform's "Bella" and "Adam" voices. The actors say those voices captured every aspect of their style, and that ElevenLabs misappropriated their likeness, their publicity rights, and violated copyright law (DMCA anti-circumvention) because voice training used copyrighted works.

The lawsuit asks ElevenLabs to stop creating and distributing voices modeled on those actors, to notify users not to create clones, and to pay profits plus damages. Even after "Bella" was removed, the voice continues to appear across thousands of YouTube videos — showing how difficult it is to truly retract a stolen voice once it is in the world.

ElevenLabs is not uniquely evil. It is a clean example of the pattern:

- **Scrape first.** Take voice data without clear, upfront consent.
- **Sort out rights later.** Treat licensing and attribution as legal cleanup, not design.
- **Call it "innovation."** Frame extraction as progress.
- **Outsource the harm to creators.** Creators lose income, control, and identity.

The structural damage is this: once your voice is in a model, it is no longer fully yours. You can win a lawsuit. You cannot un-train the model. You cannot pull your voice out of the videos it already seeded. The harm is not a bug in one company's behavior — it is what happens when consent is not infrastructure.

**This is what NOIZY inverts:**

- Sound stays tied to its story via `.noizy.provenance.json`.
- Creators keep withdrawal rights through the One-Click Takedown Ledger.
- Usage and credit are visible in the NOIZY Ledger, not hidden behind trade secrets.
- Consent is checked before synthesis, not cleaned up afterward.

The ElevenLabs case is not a cautionary tale about one bad actor. It is a preview of the default future — unless the infrastructure is built differently from the start.

## 4. What NOIZY stands for

NOIZY is built on four practical principles:

1. **Sound stays tied to its story.**
   Every asset carries a `.noizy.provenance.json` sidecar. You cannot have the sound without its intent, origin, and license.

2. **Creators keep intent and withdrawal rights.**
   You have the right to know how your work is used, and the right to withdraw it from future use. The **One-Click Takedown Ledger** makes that possible.

3. **Compensation and usage are visible.**
   Value flows are not hidden behind trade secrets. The ledger shows who contributed what, where, and under which terms.

4. **Privacy is protected by design.**
   We collect only what's needed, keep sensitive details private, and respect deletion or access requests when future use is no longer wanted.

This is the practical opposite of a platform where creators often lack visibility into where their work goes or how value is captured.
